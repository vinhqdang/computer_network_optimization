# Referee report R2: realism, related work, baselines, experiments, presentation

Manuscript: "Priority Now, Payback Later: Online Capacity Allocation with Bounded Class Envy for Tiered Internet Access" (REPAY).
Scope of this report: model realism for an ISP/hotspot operator, related-work coverage, baseline fairness (repay/mechanisms.py), experimental design and statistics, whether conclusions follow from the tables/figures, reproducibility, and clarity of intro/related/experiments/discussion. I did not assess the proofs in depth and did not re-run experiments. I read paper/sections/*.tex, paper/tables/*.tex, repay/{mechanisms,params,sim}.py, experiments/common.py, exp_compare.py, exp_deviation.py, and inspected fig_compare, fig_collapse, fig_deviation, fig_bound and fig_variability.

## 1. Summary

The paper proposes REPAY, a drift-plus-penalty allocation rule for a single access link. Elastic devices passed over in favour of declared real-time (RT) flows accumulate per-device "deficits" that raise their priority and the price of further RT priority in their tier. The aim is to make misdeclaring "real-time" unattractive without payments. The main results are:
(i) a deterministic, sample-path bound on every deficit and hence on "class envy";
(ii) approximate, large-market incentive statements;
(iii) an O(1/V) utility gap to a stationary benchmark;
(iv) slot-level simulations on synthetic hotspot and home traces, where REPAY is reported to keep envy at about 19 Mbit (against about 870 for plain DPP and about 2000 for WFQ/Priority), to serve about 90% of RT slots (PF: 82%) at essentially PF's elastic rate, and to remove the profit of misreporting that Priority/WFQ/plain DPP give (median +170%/+154%/+62% versus +0.2%).

The problem is well chosen and the writing is generally careful and honest. The authors flag many limits themselves: synthetic traces, a single run-in-simulation "Credit" adaptation, hold-down not enforced, thin markets. The deviation experiment (exact re-simulation of 180 unilateral deviations, plus thin-market and oracle-switching attacks) is the most convincing part and is better than most papers in this area.

My concerns are mainly with three things.
1. The experimental world is a fluid, slot-level abstraction with stylised traffic that is far from what a hotspot operator or Wi-Fi QoS engineer sees. The model's central premise ("the device declares its class and the operator cannot verify it") is not argued against how classification is done in practice.
2. The baseline set is weak or mis-specified for the claim actually made (an incentive-compatible protection of RT traffic that beats simple practical rules). In particular there is no efficient-frontier comparison, no capped or rate-limited priority, and no verification or classification baseline. The business-constraint experiment is built so that the baselines cannot satisfy the constraints.
3. Statistical support is thin in several places (2 to 5 seeds, normal 1.96 CIs, unpaired intervals, correlated devices treated as independent), and several textual claims are not backed by a table or are stronger than the table.

## 2. Recommendation: MAJOR REVISION

The idea and the exact-deviation methodology deserve publication in a good venue. The empirical section, as it stands, does not support the headline claim "REPAY gives practical protection of RT traffic that cannot be gamed" against the right alternatives, and the realism gap is too large for the networking audience without either extra experiments or a considerably more modest framing. Items M1 to M5 are required. M6 to M9 are strongly recommended.

## 3. Major concerns

### M1. The incentive premise and the "declared class" are not tied to how operators and Wi-Fi actually work
Location: abstract, Section 1 paragraphs 2-3, Section 3 ("Service classes"), Section 7 (limits).
Problem:
- The paper says that with encrypted traffic the AP "often cannot tell what an application is" and that the class "is in effect declared by the device" (DSCP/WMM markings).
- For downlink traffic, which the paper itself restricts to, the DSCP marking is set by the server or an upstream router, not by the receiving device, and most public hotspots and ISPs rewrite or bleach DSCP at ingress.
- Operators in practice classify on port/SNI/flow-pattern heuristics, or on per-device policing.
- A bulk download "declaring video" is trivially detected by its behaviour: it is greedy and persistent, whereas a video call is rate-bounded at about rmax_V.
- The model gives the operator no such cheap verification, and none of the baselines uses it.
- Recent practice also has explicit signalling: the Wi-Fi Alliance QoS Management (MSCS/SCS, DSCP policy), IEEE 802.11be/Wi-Fi 7 low-latency features, IETF L4S (RFC 9330-9332), 5G QoS flows. Net-neutrality regimes (e.g. EU BEREC guidelines) restrict commercially motivated class discrimination, which is relevant to "free/premium/PAYG" classes inside one pool.
- No literature or practice evidence is given that device-declared RT classes are actually abused at hotspots. The abstract and intro state it as a fact.
Fix:
- Add a short "operational setting" subsection saying which markings are device-controlled (uplink WMM/DSCP, VPN/proxy tunnelled flows, QUIC) and which are not.
- Cite measurements of DSCP abuse or classification accuracy, or state explicitly that the threat model is hypothetical.
- Add a baseline that uses cheap behavioural verification (per-device RT rate cap/policer, or demotion to elastic when rate persistently exceeds rmax or when not rate-bounded), and show what REPAY adds on top. See M3.

### M2. The traffic and network model is too stylised to support "realistic" and "Wi-Fi" claims
Location: Section 3, Section 6.1 Setup, discussion "Modelling choices", keywords and title ("Tiered Internet Access", Wi-Fi).
Problems:
1. Capacity is an exogenous scalar C_t independent of who is served and of their PHY rates. There is no airtime accounting, no multi-rate anomaly, no contention overhead, no OFDMA/MU effects. The elastic devices' link rates are uniform on [5,30] Mbit/s but play no role except as a demand cap. For a paper that invokes Wi-Fi resource management this is the first thing a Wi-Fi referee will object to. Airtime fairness (the paper cites Høiland-Jørgensen 2017) is precisely the issue that makes "rate" the wrong resource.
2. A device has exactly one class for its whole sojourn. In reality a laptop runs a call and a download concurrently, and classification is per flow. 35% of all devices in the hotspot are in a video call or a game for their entire stay (4 min mean), which is implausibly high.
3. Elastic devices are saturated whenever "on" (cap equals link rate, 5-30 Mbit/s). With about 75 devices in 91 Mbit/s the link is always oversubscribed. Real hotspot traffic is dominated by short, finite, interactive web/app transfers whose completion time, not volume, matters. The assumption that elastic users value only cumulative volume and "being repaid later costs them nothing" is crucial to the design but untested; it would fail for web browsing and for any transfer that ends when the user leaves. The mean sojourn is only 4 minutes.
4. Demand and population are exogenous (no TCP/congestion control, no user departure when service is poor, no transfer completion). The discussion acknowledges this, but the headline numbers (RT success, elastic rate, envy in Mbit) are then not meaningful in absolute terms.
5. RT success is a per-second binary at >= rmin. Rotation of admission inside an RT group (needed for the incentive result, see algorithm and discussion) means that when a < 1 individual calls are cut for whole seconds in turns. A video call or game with 10% random one-second outages, or alternating on/off every few seconds, is not "served 90%". No outage-length distribution, number of interruptions per call, or QoE/MOS proxy is reported. The discussion has one sentence on this.
6. Gaming at 0.2 Mbit/s fixed rate with utility 1 is not representative of gaming (about 0.05-0.5 Mbit/s but latency-critical). Latency is absent from the model although it is the defining requirement of the class.
Fix:
- Add at least one experiment that uses a more realistic substrate: an ns-3 or packet-level Wi-Fi/queue model (even a single AP with a handful of stations), or a measured trace for capacity and for flow arrivals (public hotspot or ISP flow logs exist).
- Otherwise remove "Wi-Fi" from keywords and positioning and call it a fluid single-bottleneck model.
- Add per-flow (mixed) classes at least in a sensitivity run.
- Add RT outage-run-length metrics and elastic transfer-completion-time metrics, using finite-size elastic transfers.
- Report sensitivity to the fraction of RT devices (e.g. 5%, 10%, 35%), to sojourn length, to load (mean fair share), and to link-rate heterogeneity.

### M3. Baselines are weak, partly strawman, and the key comparison (Pareto frontier) is missing
Location: Section 6.1 "Baselines", Table 2, Figure 3, Section 6.3, repay/mechanisms.py.
Problems:
1. The claim "REPAY sits between the two on RT success while giving elastic devices what PF gives" is a statement about a point, not a frontier. WFQ is run with a single arbitrary RT weight multiplier (4, mechanisms.py `WFQ(rt_mult=4.0)`); plain DPP and REPAY use a single V (20). A WFQ multiplier of, say, 1.5-2 might deliver about 90% RT success at an elastic rate close to 1.7 Mbit/s. I cannot tell from the paper. Likewise V is swept only for the bound and optimality gap, not for the comparison. Without RT-success versus elastic-rate (and versus volume lost) curves obtained by sweeping WFQ multiplier, plain-DPP V, REPAY V and Karma parameters, "44% of the gap closed at 1.7% lower elastic rate" is not evidence of dominance.
2. WFQ and Priority are known to be gameable, and the paper uses them mainly as foils for the incentive result. The natural practical countermeasures are not included: (a) priority with a per-device RT rate cap/policer, which is already implicit in the declared-class cap at rmax_V but not exploited; (b) priority with a bounded share of capacity for the RT class (as in Cake's diffserv tins, WMM/EDCA with admission control, 5G GBR limits); (c) priority with admission control limited by per-tier RT quotas; (d) DRR/FQ-CoDel/Cake-like flow-queueing with sparse-flow optimisation, the de facto fix for latency-sensitive traffic on Linux APs (airtime-fair mac80211 queues, cited only in passing). (a)/(b) in particular would reduce the profit of lying to a bounded amount without any deficit machinery, and the paper's finding that REPAY beats Priority/WFQ on incentives is then partly an artefact of comparing against uncapped schemes. The Priority implementation has no per-slot or cumulative RT quota.
3. "Credit" is a class-blind Karma adaptation (the paper says so). In mechanisms.py it has no guaranteed fraction alpha, no distinction between donated and borrowed credit, no cap, and RT flows are treated like any other device. It can therefore not protect RT flows by construction; the "Credit" row (RT 82.6%, video 75.7%, below PF; utility 64.4, lowest) is evidence of the adaptation, not of Karma. The comparison to the closest prior art, the thing the related-work section says is "not new", is therefore the weakest comparison in the paper. A fairer test is Karma with class-aware demand: RT flows' demand valued at a premium or treated as bursts against accumulated credits, or at least Karma with its published parameters.
4. Plain DPP (RT 92.2%, utility 72.1) and the "No-debt" variant (RT 91.0%, envy 28 Mbit, median deviation gain 0.7%, pooled -7.9%) both dominate or tie REPAY on RT success and utility in Table 2. No-debt achieves most of the incentive benefit in Table 4 (CI of median [-0.1, 1.0]) with envy 28 against 19. The paper's narrative needs to say plainly that per-device deficits are the active ingredient and the debt price adds a modest increment, and give evidence for the latter (a significance test or CIs on the difference).
5. The business-constraint experiment (Section 6.6, Table 7) is constructed so that baselines fail: weights are set to 1/1/1 and commitments are raised so that PF/WFQ/Priority/Credit deliver 0.55 of the premium commitment against a 0.8 target. An operator who promises a premium rate would simply raise w_P (or use committed-rate weights) in WFQ/PF. In the default configuration, which uses the operator's weights, every rule meets every target with margin (premium ratio 1.15-1.44 against 0.8, free floor 2.6-3.3 against 1). So the constraint machinery shows no benefit where it is not artificially starved of help, and plain DPP gets the same constraint results as REPAY, so this is not a REPAY contribution. A fair baseline is PF/WFQ with the minimum weight that meets the targets (tuned on a separate seed set).
6. No baseline from the learning/online-optimisation literature the paper cites (Si Salem et al.'s long-term fairness, Balseiro-style dual descent, DRL schedulers for Wi-Fi/5G slicing that have been popular 2023-2026) is run, although the related-work section positions REPAY relative to them.
Fix:
- Add frontier plots: RT success (and RT outage-run-length) versus elastic volume, for WFQ(m) with m in {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}, DPP and REPAY over V in {2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100}, and Credit with several fair-share fractions.
- Add the capped/quota-limited priority baselines and an FQ-CoDel/Cake-style baseline (at least a flow-queueing fluid approximation).
- Add a class-aware Karma baseline using published parameters.
- Re-do the tier experiment with tuned-weight baselines.
- State explicitly in text and abstract that PF/WFQ with ad hoc weights satisfy constraints in the default configuration.

### M4. Statistical treatment is thin and sometimes incorrect
Location: Section 6.1 "Metrics" (CI definition and seed counts), experiments/common.py `mean_ci`, Sections 6.2-6.6, Tables 2, 3, 6, 7.
Problems:
1. `mean_ci` uses 1.96 σ/√n regardless of n. With 3 seeds (optimality gap, default business constraints) and 5 seeds (bound, variability, binding table) the 95% interval is far too narrow (the t multiplier is 4.30 for n=3 and 2.78 for n=5). With n=2 (switching and oracle attacks, 20 devices per trace) no CI is given at all.
2. The intervals compare mechanisms run on the same traces, but the text compares them as if independent. "Higher utility than PF" (70.7 ± 2.2 against 68.7 ± 2.3) is not significant on unpaired intervals; the paired difference may be significant, but it is not reported. The same applies to "within three points" (REPAY versus DPP), to "REPAY-E differs only in the third decimal", and to "dropping the debt price raises RT success by one point". Report paired differences with CIs.
3. The 40 devices (oracle/switching) and the 30 devices per trace (deviation) are correlated within a trace (shared capacity path and shared population). The deviation table correctly uses a cluster bootstrap over traces, but with only 6 clusters the percentile bootstrap is unreliable (CI "[-0.5, 0.2]" for a point estimate of 0.2 with the point at the upper edge illustrates this). The claims "at most 23% of devices gain" and "no device exceeds the bound" in the switching/oracle experiments rest on 2 traces.
4. The deviator sample is drawn among elastic devices with sojourn >= 60 slots (pick_devices: `L >= 60`). The bound's practical relevance is greatest for short stays (B/L per slot, as the discussion itself states), and those devices are exactly excluded. With mean sojourn 240 and geometric distribution a significant fraction of devices is excluded. Run a stratified sample including short stays (e.g. 5-60 slots).
5. Run length and warm-up: traces start empty (no burn-in is mentioned), T = 6000 for the comparison and only 3000 for the deviation study (about 12 mean sojourns, with the first minutes in transient). Cap the starting transient or report with warm-up discarded.
6. "Never exceeds 0.43" (bound) and "0.21-0.34 of the bound" are maxima over a handful of seeds and two scenarios, which is a weak form of testing a worst-case guarantee. The test is more informative as a stress test: random adversarial capacity processes, adversarial arrival bursts, optimisation of capacity paths to maximise D/B (a search or an adversary), not hand-picked square waves.
Fix: use Student-t (or bootstrap) intervals with >= 20 seeds for the comparison/tier/variability tables (the runtime is small: 2 ms/slot), 10-20 traces for the deviation study, paired differences, report effect sizes, and add an adversarial-search stress test for the bound.

### M5. Several claims go beyond what the experiments show
1. Abstract: "REPAY serves 90% of real-time slots against 82% for proportional fairness at the same delay-tolerant throughput." True of the point estimates (1.75 vs 1.78), but the abstract omits that plain DPP serves 92% and WFQ 99% and Priority 100%, and that the protection is partial: a 10% second-level outage rate. See M2.5.
2. Section 6.3: "closes 44% of the gap in RT success ... with a higher utility than PF". The utility difference is not shown to be significant (M4.2). More importantly, the "utility" is the unweighted sum of per-slot true utilities, with RT utility u_v = 1+log(z/rmin) in [1, 2.8] and elastic log(1+x). The cross-class exchange rate is arbitrary and drives the ranking (Priority and WFQ have the highest utility: 74.8, 73.9 against REPAY 70.7). The text sets this aside ("their defects are envy and incentives"), but utility is also cited as REPAY's advantage over PF. Pick one reading and show the ranking is robust to the relative weight of RT versus elastic utility, or drop utility as a headline metric.
3. "The gain is in the free tier: 83% of RT slots against 70%; the paid tiers are served in more than 99% of slots by every rule that uses the tier weights". There is no table or figure with RT success per tier (the numbers exist in results/compare.json, `rt_success` per tier). Add it.
4. Section 6.3, variability: at the deepest shortage level (s = 1.25) REPAY (71.8 ± 2.1), PF (70.5 ± 1.6), plain DPP (72.4) and Credit (71.7) are indistinguishable, and REPAY gives no RT benefit over PF. The text says so, but the abstract and conclusion do not; they should state the regime of benefit (moderate, transient shortage).
5. Section 6.4 "unilateral deviations". The selection of "video" as the single deviation target, with the elastic device's declared-video cap at 3 Mbit/s, makes the deviation unattractive for high-rate devices when capacity is plentiful (the authors say so). This makes REPAY, PF and Credit look better than they are for deviators with high fair share. Add (a) gaming declaration (low rate, hard to cap, always served first in Priority), (b) a deviation that is optimal rather than fixed (best-response over a menu: "video when congested, otherwise elastic" is only a heuristic; an optimisation-based or RL best response over declarations in an exact simulation, even on a small instance, is the real test), and (c) coalitions or batches of deviators in a thin market. The collapse experiment with a fraction rho covers (c) only partly, with 4 traces.
6. The conclusion "the truthful profile rho = 0 is stable" under REPAY is drawn from a one-sided comparison at rho = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 (Figure 6, middle) with 4 traces and a very small gap (-0.18 Mbit/s). State it as an empirical observation in this simulator.
7. Section 6.4, reverse deviation and the 40-device runs: counts without CIs.
8. "Class envy ... computed in the same way for every rule" (Section 6.1): the external envy metric uses the mean rate of the whole RT group, which for a rule that serves a random subset (admission fraction) is the expected, not realised, reference; verify that the envy-free baseline PF (0) and rules with rotation are measured on equal footing, and say what the "reference" is when there is no RT flow in the tier (reference 0).
9. Section 1 and Related Work: "To our knowledge ... there is no drift-plus-penalty or constrained-online-learning result with guarantees per agent over a finite sojourn". The search described ("venues and preprint servers ... up to November 2026") is not reproducible. Soften to "we did not find", or provide the search protocol in an appendix.

### M6. Missing experiments a referee will require
1. Frontier and sensitivity sweeps (M3.1): V, w_RT multiplier, class mix, load, sojourn length, number of devices (scalability of the incentive result as n grows from 10 to 1000: the large-market claim should show the gain decreasing with n, as the thin-market table does for L but not directly for n), tier mix, rmin/rmax of video, committed rates.
2. Robustness of the guarantee to model violations: demand caps that react to allocation, mid-stay tier changes, measurement error in d_{i,t} (the discussion says the AP measures it for downlink, but there is no experiment with noisy or inflated demand), class declared per flow not per device, devices leaving to avoid being deprived.
3. A run with an externally derived trace (public Wi-Fi flow records, MAWI/CAIDA aggregate for arrivals, an LTE/Wi-Fi capacity trace). Even a replay of one real trace as a sanity check would help a lot.
4. Operational metrics for an operator: revenue under the PAYG constraint (note that `varpi = 0` in params.py, so the PAYG revenue term of the model is switched off in all experiments and the price p is irrelevant; the text on revenue in Section 3 is thus never exercised), churn proxies, and per-tier QoE. "Network economics": there is no comparison with priced RT classes (the standard industry alternative, and what the related-work section says the paper replaces with time), so the question "why not simply charge for the RT class?" is unanswered. At least a stylised comparison of operator revenue/welfare under priced priority versus REPAY on the same traces is needed to justify "without any payment changing hands".
5. Ablation of the rotation rule and call-continuity cost (which the discussion says the incentive argument needs): measure interruptions per call and outage-run lengths with rotation, with the earlier incumbents-first rounding, and with long-period rotation.
6. Scalability beyond 3000 devices and multiple APs/backhaul is only mentioned in the discussion. The runtime experiment is single-slot solve time; state the total state memory and the per-slot bookkeeping cost (the Python loop in `Repay.allocate` over devices for deficits and incumbents is not vectorised; the 2.2 ms/slot figure at 75 devices should be reported as a function of n for the complete step, not only the solver).

### M7. Related work: gaps and mischaracterisation risk
I cannot verify arXiv identifiers dated 2026 (zahedi2026credit 2601.17944, elokda2026ev 2604.07246, kahana2026perpetual 2605.19844, lin2026spec 2610.25067, yu2026slater 2606.31480, sarkar2026geometric, abdullah2026dynamic, rezaei2026robust); research/lit contains verification notes. A reader must be able to check them: add DOIs or stable URLs, and mark preprints as such (done in bib notes, not in the text).
Observations:
1. The networking side of the related work stops around 2017 for Wi-Fi/QoS (Høiland-Jørgensen 2017). Missing 2020-2026 networking literature a referee will expect:
   - airtime-fair queueing and Cake (Høiland-Jørgensen et al.), and the Wi-Fi performance anomaly (Heusse et al. 2003);
   - L4S and low-latency DOCSIS (RFC 9330-9332, 2023) as the standards-track answer to "protect latency-sensitive traffic without classification";
   - Wi-Fi QoS Management (MSCS/SCS), 802.11ax/be scheduling, OFDMA RU allocation and TWT for latency and fairness, multi-link operation;
   - 5G/6G network slicing and SLA-constrained schedulers, including RL-based schedulers;
   - learned traffic classification of encrypted flows (which weakens the "operator cannot verify" premise, see M1);
   - practical priority-abuse and net-neutrality/zero-rating work.
   The related-work section is dominated by theory (online allocation, mechanism design without money) and thin on the systems alternatives that an operator would actually deploy.
2. Mischaracterisation risks to check:
   - sisalem2023enabling is keyed 2023 but the bib entry says 2022 (PACMMCAOMS 6(3)); fix the key/year consistency.
   - Karma (OSDI'23) is described as "single elastic resource ... proves online strategy-proofness"; Karma's guarantee is stated for its specific credit rules and utility model, and the paper's "Credit" baseline is not Karma; the text of Section 6.1 admits it, but Table 1 lists "Credit mechanisms [karma, zahedi]" with "IC without money: partial", while the Credit baseline in the experiments is not IC for RT at all (it ignores classes).
   - Table 1 marks "Drift-plus-penalty" as having "no churn" and "fairness check", and "Credit mechanisms" as "churn: partial". The ticks are subjective; define the columns (what exactly counts as churn support) or drop the table.
   - "Priority pricing ... payments" is the right foil but there is no engagement with the empirical literature on whether paid priority or time-based screening is actually preferred by operators/users (e.g. data-plan throttling, zero rating).
3. The positioning relative to Jaramillo and Srikant 2011 (per-flow deficit counters for elastic/real-time coexistence), the most similar networking paper, is a single sentence; a baseline or at least a feature-by-feature comparison of their rule in the same simulator would be fair, since it is the closest networking prior art and is easy to implement.

### M8. Reproducibility
Strengths: public repository, one script per experiment, JSON results, seeds fixed, tests, README with reproduction commands, honest note about the deviation reuse/reproduction check.
Problems:
1. No pinned environment (README says `pip install numpy scipy cvxpy matplotlib pytest` without versions); results may differ with the conic solver version for the optimality-gap benchmark. Add `requirements.txt`/lockfile, Python version, hardware and wall-clock time for each script.
2. The paper does not state the exact number of seeds and traces in one table; it is scattered across the Metrics paragraph (10, 5, 3, 4, 6, 2) and inconsistent depth. Add one table "experiment, scenario, T, seeds, devices sampled, metrics".
3. Several parameter choices are fixed without rationale: C_STAY = 0.97, multipliers (1, 0.6, 0.2), sigma = 0.15, p_on_off/p_off_on, burst size/period, tier weights (1, 2, 1.5), committed rates, theta, phi, V = 20. The discussion says they were chosen "before the comparison runs and not tuned afterwards", but the git history shows the algorithm itself (rounding rule: incumbents-first replaced by rotation) was changed after seeing deviation results; the paper says so for thin markets. A pre-registration-style statement of what was tuned on which seeds, and a held-out seed set for the final tables, would increase credibility.
4. Timing is measured with `time.perf_counter()` in a Python simulator with multiprocessing; state the CPU and that other jobs ran concurrently (`pmap` with 4 processes).

### M9. Practical deployability of "priority on credit" is not argued
Location: intro, discussion.
Problems:
1. Deficits are per device and are forfeited when the device leaves or "leaves the elastic class and returns" (mechanisms.py `_sync` resets D on class change and on departure). A device that disassociates and re-associates (MAC randomisation, which is the default on modern phones) resets its deficit; likewise a device that leaves and rejoins can reset an unfavourable position. The paper mentions forfeiting on class change but not identity: with randomised MAC addresses and short hotspot visits, per-device state is hard to maintain, and a device can obtain a fresh state, a Sybil-like channel the theory does not model (a "new arrival" has D=0 and in a paid tier a fresh login).
2. The elastic users are told nothing; the repayment is invisible, the "debt" is never actually repaid to those who leave (bounded by B, 43 Mbit for free-tier video, comparable to 10-15% of a typical device's volume in the simulation). The normative claim "being repaid later costs them nothing" should be quantified as volume lost at departure.
3. Implementation inside an AP (per-slot convex solve over all devices plus per-device state and a one-second control loop) versus the existing hierarchical qdiscs: no prototype, no discussion of integration with the mac80211 queueing layer or a gateway shaper. The "prototype on a real access point" is left as future work; given the networking venue this is a notable gap. A small Linux prototype (tc/nftables or a user-space shaper on a router, even with iperf/VoIP traffic) would greatly raise credibility.

## 4. Minor concerns

1. Abstract is long (about 20 lines) and carries many numbers; the sentence beginning "We prove that every deficit is bounded..." has four semicolon-separated claims. Shorten and state the regime where REPAY helps and where it does not (permanent shortage).
2. "Class envy" is a new term defined in volume; make it clear in the intro what envy 19 Mbit means for a user (seconds of 1 Mbit/s service lost) and compare with the volume a device receives over its stay.
3. Table 2 is too wide (11 columns, `\resizebox{\linewidth}`), with a font that is likely about 6 pt. Split into two tables (service and fairness; business ratios and envy) and drop REPAY-WC/REPAY-E rows (they are identical to REPAY to three digits and are explicitly unexercised), moving them to the appendix.
4. The service ratios in Table 2 (premium 1.37, PAYG 1.31, free floor 2.80) exceed 1, while the targets are 0.8/0.6/1: the ratio is served volume over committed demand, which can exceed 1 because RT flows are served up to rmax > committed rate. Define the ratio, explain why values above 1 are meaningful, and consider reporting the achieved fraction of the target instead. As written, the reader cannot tell whether the constraints bind (they never do in the default configuration).
5. "Utility" units are undefined in Table 2; the text says "unweighted sum of true utilities", whereas the model's objective is weighted (w_g). Say which is reported (the optimality gap section says "utility here is the operator's weighted objective"), and use distinct names.
6. Figure 3: the yellow "Credit" bars/markers (and in Figure 6, the yellow Credit lines, grey No-debt) have very low contrast on white; panel (d) uses a log axis from 0.1 where PF's zero envy cannot be shown (the caption says so, but an arrow or "0" label would help). Panel labels, fonts and legends are very small at column width.
7. Figure 5 (right panel): "off-axis: 2 below, 0 above" is unreadable at the printed size, and the "bound B_g" dashed lines for the three tiers overlap with the points; clarify which bound is plotted per tier. In Figure 5 (left), the unlabelled box-plot whisker convention (symlog axis) should be stated in the caption.
8. Figure 6 (collapse), three panels with six lines and error bars at 4 seeds, are crowded; the markers for REPAY and REPAY-E are indistinguishable; use fewer variants. State the number of seeds in the caption.
9. Figures 7 and 9 occupy 45% of the line width with large white space; combine them with another figure or put them in the appendix.
10. The label "Priority" is used both for an incentive-incompatible rule and for a rule with admission control; since admission control changes its behaviour at rho = 1 (95.8% RT success), explain in the baselines paragraph that it is a strong form of priority.
11. "REPAY-WC" and "REPAY-E" are introduced in Section 4 (variants) but are shown to be identical or never exercised; if they are not exercised in any experiment remove them from the tables or add a scenario that exercises them (e.g. a tier with no RT flows, which is common in the home scenario and in small hotspots).
12. Home scenario (Table 3): all rules serve at least 97% of RT slots and premium ratios are identical; the scenario does not discriminate among mechanisms except in envy. Either change it (lower capacity, 3-5 devices with concurrent calls) or drop it.
13. Section 6.2: the optimality-gap instance has 9 flows, slack tier constraints, i.i.d. capacity from 200 values; the statement "whole value of time-shifting is 0.35" is specific to that instance. Say so and give a second instance with binding tier constraints (the main novelty of the benchmark).
14. Section 6.2: "gap times V lies between 1.96 and 3.08 ... consistent with 1/V". A 1/V slope check with a log-log fit and CI would be better than a product range; note that for V >= 5 the product varies by 57%.
15. The text sometimes switches between "seeds" and "traces" and "devices"; define once (a seed = one independent trace shared by all mechanisms).
16. The footnote GitHub URL is non-anonymous; if the journal is double-blind, remove or anonymise. Also state a licence and a tagged release/Zenodo DOI for the artefact.
17. Typos/consistency: "Si Salem" is cited as `sisalem2023enabling` with year 2022 in bib; "Section~\ref{sec:baselines-theory}" refers to the end of a theory section for a claim about regimes, which the reader may not find; Table 5 caption defines n_E, n_V but not the capacity pattern (put "0.3 and 1 Mbit/s per device" and the alternation period in the caption).
18. Thin-market Table 5 mixes positive constants (about 5 Mbit) and negative growing values (down to -1200); explain why the deviator's loss grows with L in the n_E=0 rows (the device is capped at 3 Mbit/s when alone?) so the table is not misread.
19. The statement that the thin-market gain of about 5 Mbit is "an eighth of the bound B_{F,v} = 43 Mbit" invites the reader to ask how tight the bound is; say that observed worst-case deficit/bound is at most 0.43 and discuss whether a smaller B (smaller V) trades off.
20. Running time: "forty bisection steps per slot ... 1.4-2.0 ms for 10 to 3000 devices" is essentially flat in n, which suggests a fixed Python overhead dominates; the comparison with a conic solver on a different code path is not like for like. Report a specialised convex solver or an LP/QP active-set baseline.
21. The discussion says the comparisons use values "chosen before the comparison runs and not tuned afterwards"; since the paper changed the rounding rule after seeing results, rephrase precisely.
22. Language: mostly clear and restrained. A few places say "we show" where the evidence is simulation only (introduction: "we show that strict priority and standard utility-maximising schedulers both have this defect"; this is an empirical statement for one parameter set plus a proposition; qualify). The term "RT" is defined in Section 3 but used in the abstract/intro without definition in the abstract.
23. Add confidence intervals or counts to the sentences quoting single numbers (e.g. "$1.60$ against $0.63$ Mbit/s under Priority at rho = 0.25", "$1.06$ against $1.24$ Mbit/s"): these come from 4 traces.

## 5. Assessment of what is good (for the editor)
- The incentive question is formulated cleanly (class envy in volume), and the exact-deviation re-simulation is a sound way to test large-market statements. The thin-market and oracle-switching attacks, and the honest report that an earlier rounding rule failed, are examples of good practice.
- The deficit bound check (Figure 1) and the 1/V optimality-gap check against a conic solver are well designed for what they test.
- The limitations section is candid.

## 6. What would change my recommendation
Accept after revision if the authors (a) show the frontier comparison and add capped/quota priority and flow-queueing baselines, with a class-aware Karma variant; (b) repeat the main tables with >= 20 seeds, paired differences and t-based intervals; (c) add outage-run-length/QoE and finite-transfer completion metrics; (d) add one more realistic substrate (packet-level Wi-Fi model or a real trace) or restrict claims to a fluid model; (e) justify the declared-class threat model against current classification and QoS practice, and update related work accordingly.
